**Older Homes**
Older homes from this era and earlier may have been constructed or repaired using materials now known to pose potential health risks, including, but not limited to, asbestos-containing products and lead-based paint or plumbing components. These properties may also contain outdated or obsolete systems such as oil-fired heating equipment, knob-and-tube electrical wiring, polybutylene and galvanized plumbing, etc. Many of these items may not be visible and may require more invasive methods to detect. It is recommended that clients consult with appropriately licensed specialists for testing, evaluation, or further assessment of these conditions as needed.

Additionally, Older homes commonly experience some degree of settlement and movement over time as building materials age and the structure adjusts to changing soil and environmental conditions. This can result in uneven or sloping floors, minor wall irregularities, and doors or windows that may not operate perfectly. In many cases, a certain amount of unevenness is considered typical for homes of this age and does not necessarily indicate a structural failure. However, significant movement, worsening conditions, or other related concerns should be further evaluated by a qualified contractor or structural specialist as needed.

**Cosmetic Issues**
This inspection is focused on evaluating the functional condition and safety of the property’s systems and components. Cosmetic issues, such as paint, finishes, flooring, minor wall blemishes, or other aesthetic concerns, are not within the scope of this inspection and are not reported on unless they indicate a possible underlying structural or safety defect. Clients should be aware that cosmetic conditions are considered normal wear and tear and are excluded from this report.

No single main disconnect was noted at the panel or meter. Instead, power is divided between two sections of breakers. The upper section typically contains up to six larger breakers that control major appliances (such as the range, dryer, or HVAC system), and one of those breakers feeds the lower section that supplies the lighting and general-use circuits. In these panels, shutting off all power requires turning off up to six breakers rather than one main switch. While this design was permitted at the time of installation under the “six disconnect rule,” it is no longer used in modern construction, and upgrades are often recommended if the panel is outdated, overloaded, or showing signs of wear.
