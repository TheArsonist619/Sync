## ⚠️ Read first: notes for this house

**Order your projects around the floor.** Your [[To Buy]] board also has a kitchen wall removal, an island and painting. A sensible sequence:
1. **Remove the kitchen wall,** then patch the subfloor where it stood so the new floor can run straight through.
2. **Install the island and any cabinets.** A floating floor must never be pinned under fixed cabinets or islands. The floor goes *around* them with an expansion gap (LifeProof).
3. **Paint the walls and ceilings** while there's no new floor to protect. Paint the baseboards or quarter round after the floor is in.
4. **Then lay the flooring.**

**The kitchen floor is laminate** (see the [[Inspection Report#10. Kitchen|inspection report]]). Laminate, carpet and cushioned vinyl all have to come out, because manufacturers don't allow click LVP over them.

**There may be asbestos under the carpet (1972 house).** EPA lists vinyl floor tiles, the backing on vinyl sheet flooring, and flooring adhesives as places asbestos may be found. If you pull up carpet and find old vinyl tile, sheet vinyl or black adhesive:
- **Don't sand, dry-sweep, dry-scrape, drill, saw or chip it.** LifeProof and the flooring industry's work-practices guide (RFCI) both say to presume it contains asbestos unless testing shows otherwise.
- **Easiest path: leave it and float the LVP over it.** LifeProof approves asbestos tile, VCT and non-cushioned sheet vinyl as a base if it's a single layer, firmly attached and flat. MSI also allows intact, well-secured non-cushioned vinyl.
- **If it has to come out,** have an accredited professional take samples first (EPA doesn't recommend sampling it yourself), and use an abatement contractor if it tests positive.

**"LVP is 100% waterproof" (from [[Flooring Search]]) is only partly true.** The *planks* are waterproof, but the installed floor isn't watertight. Water can get through seams and gaps at the edges to the subfloor. LifeProof's own guide says the floor "should not be used to seal an existing floor from moisture" and can't prevent mold or damage from flooding or a wet subfloor. Wipe up spills, and seal edges at tubs and toilets with 100% silicone.

**Flatness is the big unknown,** as Fletcher Floors warned. Plan on time and money for leveling once the carpet is up (see [[#Step 2 – Prep the subfloor]]).

## Types of LVP

"Snap-on" LVP is a **click-lock floating floor**. The planks lock to each other and the floor rests on the subfloor without glue or nails, so it can expand and contract.

| Type | Core | Underfoot | Strengths | Weaknesses | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Rigid core: SPC** (stone-plastic composite) | Limestone, vinyl and stabilizers; very dense | Hard and cool, can sound louder | Best dent resistance; usually thinner; very stable | Colder and harder underfoot; harder to cut | Kitchens, entries, basements on concrete, busy areas |
| **Rigid core: WPC** (wood/polymer-plastic composite) | Foamed composite, a few mm thicker | Softer, warmer, quieter | Comfort and noise insulation; lighter and more forgiving to install | Dents more easily under heavy point loads (furniture legs, dropped items) | Bedrooms, living rooms, upstairs |
| **Flexible glue-down** ("dry-back") | No rigid core, about 2–4 mm thick | Thin | Low profile; stable once glued | Needs adhesive and a very smooth subfloor because every bump shows through; not click-lock | Mostly pros and commercial jobs |

Laminate, including "waterproof" laminate, isn't vinyl. It has a wood-fiber core, and your contractor advised against it.

### What to compare when shopping
- **Wear layer (mil):** this matters more than core type for scratches and scuffs. Rule of thumb: 12 mil is standard residential, **20 mil** suits kitchens, kids, pets and busy rooms, and 28 mil+ is commercial grade. A 20 mil WPC will out-wear a 12 mil SPC.
- **Total thickness isn't a quality score.** A 4 mm SPC plank with a 20 mil wear layer will outlast a 7 mm WPC plank with a 6 mil wear layer.
- **Attached pad:** most click LVP comes with a foam or cork pad already attached. **Don't add a second pad** unless the manufacturer approves it. LifeProof approves only its own Universal Underlayment and warns that failures caused by other underlayments may not be covered.
- **Locking system:** "drop-lock" (fold-down) systems like LifeProof's are the easiest for first-timers. Other systems angle in on both edges.
- **"Waterproof" vs "water-resistant":** read the spec sheet, because cheaper SPC products are sometimes only water-resistant.
- **Installation rules:** read the install guide *before* buying. Flatness tolerance, expansion gap and transition rules differ by brand (see [[#Manufacturer specs compared]]).
- **Sun:** long-term direct sun can fade, discolor or buckle vinyl, so use blinds or UV film on sunny windows (LifeProof).

## Step 1 – Remove the carpet

Based on [This Old House's guide](https://www.thisoldhouse.com/flooring/how-to-remove-carpet) (updated May 2026) and Home Repair Tutor's video (below).

1. **Peek underneath first.** Pull up a corner with pliers and peel back the pad to see the subfloor and any old flooring. If you find vinyl tile, sheet vinyl or black adhesive, stop and read the asbestos note above.
2. **Clear the room.** Remove furniture and take off doors that swing into the room, including bi-fold closet doors.
3. **Pull the baseboards** for clean access to the carpet edges and tack strips, and **number each piece** so it goes back in the same spot. Alternatively, keep the baseboards and finish with quarter round (see Overanalytical DIY's video).
4. **Free the edges.** Starting in a corner, pull the carpet off the tack strips all the way around the room. If the corner won't budge, cut out a 6" square and pull from there.
5. **Cut it into strips.** Fold back about 3 ft and cut along the fold from the back side, making **3–4 ft wide strips**. Roll each one tightly and tape it.
6. **Closets and transitions:** cut the carpet at closet doorways and remove that piece separately. At metal transition strips, cut along the strip.
7. **Remove the pad.**
   - **Wood subfloor:** the pad is usually stapled. Pull it up by hand, then remove the leftover staples with a scraper or pliers.
   - **Concrete:** the pad is usually glued. Scrape off the leftover chunks and adhesive in sections.
8. **Pry up the tack strips** (all of them for a hard floor). Wear heavy gloves. Start at the end nail and lever the strip up, resting the pry bar on a scrap of wood so it doesn't dent the subfloor.
9. **Inspect and clean.** Look for water stains, mold and soft spots. Vacuum thoroughly and sweep with a magnet for staples. Use a dry mop on wood, and a damp mop on concrete only for adhesive residue. Let everything dry completely.
10. **Disposal:** ask your waste hauler or carpet retailers about carpet recycling. Otherwise cut the carpet into pieces that meet local pickup rules.

## Step 2 – Prep the subfloor

This is where most failures start. Click joints are brittle, and dips or humps can crack or separate them.

1. **Fix squeaks now.** With the floor bare, drive subfloor screws through into the joists anywhere the floor creaks or moves. It's much harder once the new floor is down (see Home RenoVision's squeaky-floor video).
2. **Repair damage.** Replace soft or water-damaged subfloor sections. Particleboard, common in older homes, swells badly if it gets wet.
3. **Check flatness.** Lay a long straightedge, or stretch a string tight across the room from a nail at the center (MSI's method), and look for highs and lows.
   - **LifeProof:** within ¼" over 10 ft, with no abrupt bumps.
   - **MSI:** within 3/16" over 10 ft.
   - Sand or grind high spots, and fill low spots with floor patch or self-leveler.
4. **Test moisture.**
   - **Wood:** below 14% on a moisture meter (LifeProof).
   - **Concrete:** test before every install. LifeProof allows up to 85% relative humidity (RH probe, ASTM F2170) or under 8 lb moisture emission (calcium chloride test, ASTM F1869). MSI requires a moisture barrier above 80% RH. Test near exterior doors, plumbing walls, foundation walls and the middle of the room.
5. **Vapor barrier on concrete,** when tests or the basement's history call for it: 6-mil poly sheeting with seams **overlapped 8"** and taped (MSI). LifeProof doesn't require it but recommends it in basements prone to moisture. The inspector couldn't see most of the basement walls and floor and suggested asking the seller about past water ([[Inspection Report#8. Interior Room Assessments|inspection report]]).
6. **Undercut door jambs and casings** so the planks slide underneath. Lay a scrap plank on the floor as a guide and cut with a flush handsaw, oscillating tool or jamb saw. **Never undercut metal door jambs.** Leave a gap there and fill it with silicone (LifeProof).
7. **Clean it spotless.** No staples, nail heads, debris or adhesive ridges.

## Step 3 – Install the LVP

Steps follow the LifeProof guide. Check your own product's guide for its numbers.

**Before the first plank**
1. **Acclimation:** many rigid-core floors don't need it. LifeProof needs none, as long as the room is 50–100°F during install. MSI needs none in normal conditions, but if the boxes were stored below 40°F or above 95°F, lay them flat in the room for at least 12 hours.
2. **Plan the layout.** Planks usually run along the room's longest dimension. Measure across the room so the last row won't be a thin sliver; if it would be, trim the first row narrower to even them out.
3. **Check the starting wall.** Walls in old houses are rarely straight. Snap a chalk line, and trim (scribe) the first row to the wall if needed so the rows stay straight.
4. **Mix planks from several boxes** so color and pattern variation look natural (MSI). Set aside any damaged planks.

**Laying the floor**
5. **Spacers:** place ¼" spacers (or your product's gap size) against every wall and fixed object. Leave them in until the floor is done.
6. **Row 1:** lay planks with the **tongue facing the wall**. No end piece should be shorter than **8"**. If the last piece in a row would be, shorten the first one.
7. **Row 2:** start with a plank cut to about ⅓ length so end joints are **at least 8" apart**. LifeProof has you work rows 1 and 2 together at the start, then complete each row before moving on.
8. **Lock each plank.** Angle the long edge in, lower it, and tap along the long edge with the tapping block. *Then* tap the short end down with the soft-faced mallet. Never force the short end before the long joint is fully engaged, and never hit plank edges directly.
9. **Keep end joints random,** always at least 8" apart. Avoid repeating stair-step patterns.
10. **Cutting:** score with a utility knife against a straightedge and snap the plank. You may need to cut the attached pad separately. Use a jigsaw for notches and a hole saw for pipes, and make a paper template for odd shapes.
11. **Last row:** set a plank exactly on top of the previous row, lay another against the wall (minus the gap) to mark it, then rip it to width. Use the **pull bar on long edges only**.
12. **Transitions:** use T-moldings where your product requires them. LifeProof requires them for runs over 100 linear ft and between different floor types. MSI requires them for runs over 50 ft and in doorways narrower than 4 ft. Transition pieces must not pin the floor down.
13. **Fixed objects:** keep the gap around cabinets, the island, metal door jambs and pipes. Fill gaps that trim can't cover with **100% silicone**. LVP can run under a toilet, but leave a gap at the flange, seal it with silicone, and never anchor the toilet through the floor (LifeProof).
14. **Trim:** pull the spacers, then reinstall the baseboards or add quarter round. **Nail into the wall, never the floor.**

**Aftercare:** put felt pads on all furniture legs, don't roll heavy appliances across the floor, use blinds in strong sun, and store leftover planks flat.

## Manufacturer specs compared

Two real installation guides show how much the rules vary.

| Spec | LifeProof (Home Depot, Mar 2024) | MSI hybrid rigid core |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Acclimation | Not required (install at 50–100°F) | Not required; 12+ hrs if stored below 40°F or above 95°F |
| Subfloor flatness | ¼" in 10 ft; slope ≤ 1" per 6 ft | 3/16" in 10 ft |
| Expansion gap | ¼" | 3/8" |
| T-molding required | Runs > 100 linear ft; between floor types | Runs > 50 ft; doorways < 4 ft wide |
| Concrete moisture | ≤ 85% RH or < 8 lb MVER | Moisture barrier required above 80% RH |
| Vapor barrier | Not required; recommended in damp basements | Installer's call; 6-mil poly, 8" overlaps |
| OK to install over | One layer of non-cushioned vinyl, VCT, asbestos tile, ceramic, concrete, plywood, OSB, particleboard | Intact, well-secured non-cushioned vinyl or linoleum, ceramic, wood |
| Not allowed over | Carpet, laminate, cushioned vinyl, other floating floors, cork | Carpet |
| End-joint stagger | ≥ 8" | ≥ 8" |
| Cabinets/islands on top | No; install them first | No (unless the floor is fully glued) |

## Common mistakes

All of these break a manufacturer rule above:
- **Skipping the flatness check,** which leads to cracked or separating joints.
- **Pinning the floor** with trim nails, transition screws, cabinets or an island on top, which leads to buckling.
- **Leaving no expansion gap** at walls and fixed objects.
- **Adding a second pad** under a plank that already has one.
- **Tapping the short end before the long edge is locked,** which damages the tongue and groove.
- **End joints closer than 8",** or a repeating pattern.
- **Not testing a concrete slab for moisture.**
- **Not undercutting door jambs,** which leaves an ugly gap or a pinched plank.
- **A sliver last row,** or never checking whether the starting wall is straight.
