## ⚠️ This house is from 1972: read before sanding or scraping

**Lead paint.** Homes built before 1978 may have lead-based paint, and the [[Inspection Report#1. Inspection Details|inspection report]] calls out this risk for homes of this era. Painting over intact paint is fine. Sanding, scraping or stripping old layers is what creates hazardous dust.
- **Test first.** EPA-recognized swab kits (e.g., LeadCheck) are a quick screen. For a definitive answer, EPA recommends a certified lead inspector or risk assessor. If you can't confirm, EPA says to assume lead is present.
- **If there's lead:** seal the room with plastic sheeting (cover doors and vents), mist surfaces before sanding or scraping, use wet-dry sandpaper or sanding sponges with a HEPA vacuum, wear an N-100 respirator, and wet-wipe the area daily. Full list: [EPA – Lead-Safe Renovations for DIYers](https://www.epa.gov/lead/lead-safe-renovations-diyers).

**Textured ceilings.** Some ceilings in the inspection photos look textured. Textured finishes and joint compounds from this era can contain asbestos, and you can't tell by looking. EPA's advice is to leave it alone if it's in good condition. Before you scrape, sand or remove it, have an accredited professional take samples; EPA doesn't recommend sampling it yourself. Source: [EPA – Protect Your Family from Exposures to Asbestos](https://www.epa.gov/asbestos/protect-your-family-exposures-asbestos).

## Order of work for one room

1. **Plan:** pick a color and sheen, test a sample on the wall, and work out how much paint you need (see below).
2. **Clear and protect:** move furniture out or to the center under plastic, lay canvas drop cloths, and remove cover plates, hardware and light fixtures. Bag and label the screws.
3. **Repair:** patch holes, sand smooth, caulk gaps along the trim, and spot-prime patches and stains.
4. **Clean:** vacuum the dust, then wipe the walls. Degrease kitchen and bathroom walls.
5. **Ceiling:** cut a ~3" band around the edge, roll the rest, and apply two coats.
6. **Trim, windows and doors,** if you're repainting them.
7. **Walls:** cut in one wall, roll it while the cut-in is still wet, then move to the next wall. Apply the second coat after the recoat time on the can.
8. **Baseboards** last.
9. **Finish:** pull tape, reinstall hardware, clean tools and label the leftover paint.

This is This Old House's order. Some pros paint the trim first and overlap it about ⅛" onto the wall so the wall paint covers the overlap (Fine Homebuilding). Either works.

## How much paint?

- Most interior paint covers roughly **350–400 sq ft per gallon per coat** on smooth, primed walls. Texture lowers that. Check the can.
- **Formula:** (room perimeter × wall height − ~20 sq ft per door − ~15 sq ft per window) ÷ 350 = gallons per coat. Plan on two coats.
- **Example:** a 12×12 room with 8 ft ceilings has 384 sq ft of wall. Subtract one door and two windows for about 334 sq ft, which is ~1 gallon per coat or **2 gallons total**.
- If you need several gallons, mix them together in a 5-gallon bucket ("boxing") so the color is identical wall to wall.

## Paint, sheen & primer

**Buy good paint.** Top-line paint costs more per gallon but goes on better and lasts longer (This Old House). Common picks are Sherwin-Williams Emerald or SuperPaint and Benjamin Moore Regal Select or Aura. For trim, doors and cabinets, use a waterborne alkyd or urethane enamel such as Benjamin Moore ADVANCE or Sherwin-Williams Emerald Urethane.

| Sheen | Use it for |
| --- | --- |
| Flat | Ceilings and low-traffic walls. Hides imperfections best. |
| Matte | Bedrooms and living rooms. Touches up well but is less washable. |
| Eggshell | Kitchens, bathrooms and hallways. More washable and stain-resistant. |
| Satin | Cabinets and high-traffic areas. Easy to clean. |
| Semi-gloss | Trim and doors. |
| High gloss | Front doors and accents. |

| Situation | Primer |
| --- | --- |
| Similar color over sound paint | Usually none needed |
| New drywall or plaster, patches, light color over dark | Water-based latex primer, tinted for big color changes |
| Water stains or rust | Oil-based primer |
| Crayon or grease stains | Latex stain-blocking primer |
| Bare wood trim | Oil-based primer |
| Varnished or polyurethaned trim or paneling | Shellac primer (ventilate well) |
| MDF | Shellac or oil-based (water-based primer can swell it) |
| Laminate | Bonding primer |

Sheen and primer tables are from This Old House.

## Prep (about 85% of the job)

- **Remove cover plates** instead of painting around them.
- **Fill nail holes and dings** with lightweight spackle, slightly overfilled. Sand flush once dry, then prime the patches so they don't show through as dull spots ("flashing").
- **Wider cracks** need joint compound and mesh tape.
- **Caulk trim gaps** with a small bead and smooth it with a wet finger. Don't overdo it.
- **Scuff-sand** glossy trim with 220-grit so the new paint grips. Lightly sand walls with 120-grit on a pole sander to knock down bumps.
- **Get the dust off:** vacuum, then wipe with a damp cloth before painting.
- **Light the room** with work lights, even if it gets good daylight (Fine Homebuilding).

## Technique

### Cutting in
- Pour a few inches of paint into a small pail. Dip the bristles about two fingers deep and tap off the excess (don't wipe it off).
- Hold the brush like a pencil. Start each stroke a little away from the line, then ease the bead of paint up to the edge.
- The first coat doesn't need a perfect line. Tidy it up on the second coat (This Old House).
- Cut in one wall and roll it right away. If the cut-in dries before you roll, it can leave a visible frame around the wall ("hat-banding").
- **No steady hand?** Hold a wide putty or taping knife against the edge as a guide and drag it along with the brush (This Old House). Or use tape: press the edge down firmly and pull it off at a 45° angle.

### Rolling walls
- Load the roller until it's fully covered but not dripping. Use moderate, even pressure, because pressing hard leaves ridges (This Old House).
- Start near the top beside the cut-in. Roll a "W" in roughly 3-ft sections, then fill it in.
- Finish each section with light top-to-bottom strokes, always in the same direction (Home RenoVision).
- **Keep a wet edge:** roll back into paint you just applied, never over half-dry paint. Rolling over drying paint causes lap marks (Paint Life TV).
- Make the first stroke after loading go *upward* so paint doesn't spatter onto the floor (Home RenoVision).
- Lightly roll close to the edges to blend the brush texture into the roller texture.
- Apply two coats. This Old House waits about 90 minutes between coats, but check the can.
- Before you clean up, check the walls with a work light held low at an angle.

### Ceilings
- Use flat ceiling paint. Cut a ~3" band around the edge, then roll with an extension pole and apply two coats.
- Roll in rows across the short dimension of the room so the edge stays wet.
- **Textured ceilings:** see the asbestos note at the top. If you paint one, use a thick-nap roller with light pressure and a single pass, because wet paint can loosen the texture.

### Trim, doors & baseboards
- Use semi-gloss or satin enamel. Brush thin coats with the grain.
- Take doors off their hinges and lay them flat if you can (This Old House).
- Do baseboards last: cut along the top first, then work down to the floor.
- **Original stained wood trim or paneling** (common in 1960s–70s homes): scuff-sand, prime with shellac, then paint. See the This Old House videos below.

### Kitchen cabinets
Cabinets are a different job from walls. Degrease, sand, use a bonding or shellac primer, and finish with a hard enamel. Take the doors off and label where each one goes. See the walkthroughs below.

## Pro tricks & time-savers

- **Between coats or overnight,** wrap brushes and rollers tightly in plastic wrap or a bag instead of washing them. On hot days, put them in the fridge (This Old House).
- **Line the roller tray with foil** so cleanup is just throwing the foil away.
- **De-fuzz new roller covers** by wrapping them in painter's tape and peeling it off, or use a lint roller.
- **Keep a damp rag in your pocket** for drips and wall smudges.
- **Label leftover paint** with the room, color, sheen and date. Keep it for touch-ups.
- **Matching existing paint:** look for old cans in the garage or basement first. Otherwise cut a small chip from a hidden spot and take it to a paint store. Skip phone color-matching apps (This Old House).

## Cleanup

- **Latex paint:** wash brushes in warm water with a few drops of dish soap, bristles pointing down, keeping water out of the metal ferrule.
- **Roller covers:** scrape off excess paint with the curved edge of a 5-in-1 tool before rinsing.
- **Drying and storage:** blot or spin brushes dry, then store them flat or hanging in their original sleeve. Never store them bristles-up.
- **Oil-based paint:** clean with mineral spirits instead of water.

## Common mistakes

- Skipping prep, or not priming patches, which leaves dull "flashing" spots.
- Overloading the brush, which causes drips and thick edges.
- Pressing hard or stretching the paint with the roller, which leaves ridges and thin coverage.
- Letting the cut-in dry before rolling, which leaves a visible frame.
- Rolling over half-dry paint, which causes lap marks.
- Stopping at one coat.
- Using plastic drop cloths.
- Painting in poor light.
